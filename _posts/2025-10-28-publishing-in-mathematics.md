Publishing in mathematics follows a rhythm quite different from computer science or machine learning. There are no conference deadlines driving the field, no rebuttal sprints, no leaderboard tables. A result can take years from conjecture to journal page — and that slowness is structural, not accidental. These are notes on how the process actually works, gathered during my M.Sc. and thesis years.

## 1. The arXiv comes first

In mathematics, the canonical first step is posting a preprint to the [arXiv](https://arxiv.org). This establishes priority, invites feedback, and makes the work citable immediately — often a year or more before journal publication. The practical consequences:

- **Write the preprint as if it were the journal version.** Reviewers, collaborators, and hiring committees will read this version, not the one that appears later.
- **Choose the right category** (e.g. `math.DG` for differential geometry) so the paper reaches its actual audience.
- **Version carefully.** arXiv keeps every version public; a v1 with gaps in proofs is permanently visible. Post when the arguments are complete, not when they are merely plausible.

## 2. Theorems are the unit of contribution

An ML paper argues through experiments; a mathematics paper argues through proof. That changes what "a publishable result" means:

- **One sharp theorem beats three vague ones.** A clean statement with a complete proof and a clear explanation of why it matters will always out-perform a sprawling manuscript.
- **Definitions are contributions too.** Framing the right definitions — as with discrete differential operators on meshes — is often the hardest and most valuable part.
- **Examples carry weight.** A theorem illustrated by a worked example, a computation, or (increasingly) a verified formalization is far more convincing than a bare statement-proof block.

## 3. Refereeing is slow and serious

Mathematics refereeing routinely takes six months to over a year, and a good referee will check proofs line by line. Expect:

- **Reports that engage with the mathematics**, not just the positioning. A referee may find a genuine gap — treat that as a gift, not an insult.
- **Revisions measured in months**, sometimes requiring new lemmas or restructured arguments.
- **Rejection without stigma.** Journals are highly selective and specialized; a rejection with a substantive report usually improves the paper and clarifies the right venue.

## 4. A practical checklist

1. Finish the proofs before writing a single section — write mathematics *from* completeness, not toward it.
2. Circulate a draft to two or three trusted readers before the arXiv.
3. Write the introduction last, and make it readable by a non-specialist in your area.
4. Match the journal to the result's character (specialist vs. generalist, pure vs. applied).
5. Respond to referees point by point, quoting each comment and describing exactly what changed.
6. Keep the code, computations, and (where relevant) formalizations archived alongside the paper.

The common thread: mathematics publishing rewards completeness and clarity over speed. A paper that is correct, well-framed, and honestly delimited will find its venue — and will still be read a decade later, which is the real metric.
